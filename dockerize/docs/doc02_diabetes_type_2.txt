Type 2 diabetes is a metabolic condition in which blood glucose remains too high because the body resists insulin and may not produce enough insulin over time. Insulin helps move glucose from the bloodstream into cells for energy. When insulin resistance develops, the pancreas works harder, and glucose can rise gradually for years. Symptoms may include increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision, slow wound healing, and recurrent infections, although some people have no early symptoms. Diagnosis often uses fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1c, oral glucose tolerance testing, or random glucose with symptoms. Long term complications can affect the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart, brain, and blood vessels. Management usually combines nutrition planning, regular physical activity, weight management, glucose monitoring, blood pressure control, lipid control, and medicines. Metformin is commonly used first, while GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, insulin, and other medicines may be appropriate. Foot care, eye exams, kidney monitoring, and vaccination are important. This content supports learning and should not replace professional care.

For RAG practice, useful retrieval clues include condition name, symptoms, risk factors, complications, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment. A learner should compare lifestyle measures with medicine classes and notice when urgent evaluation is needed. The assistant should answer from the document, cite the filename, and avoid giving personal dosing or replacing a clinician.
