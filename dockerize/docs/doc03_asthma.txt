Asthma is a chronic airway disease marked by inflammation, narrowing of the bronchial tubes, and variable airflow limitation. People with asthma may experience wheezing, cough, chest tightness, and shortness of breath. Symptoms can worsen at night, during exercise, with respiratory infections, or after exposure to triggers such as dust mites, pollen, mold, smoke, cold air, pollution, strong odors, and animal dander. Diagnosis is based on clinical history and lung function testing such as spirometry, which may show reversible obstruction after a bronchodilator. Asthma treatment has two main goals: controlling daily inflammation and relieving sudden symptoms. Inhaled corticosteroids are central controller medicines for many patients. Reliever medicines, often bronchodilators, open the airways during symptom flares. Good inhaler technique is essential because poor technique can make effective medicine fail. An asthma action plan helps patients recognize worsening symptoms and respond early. Severe attacks require urgent care, especially if speaking becomes difficult, lips turn blue, or reliever medicine is not helping. This document is educational only.
