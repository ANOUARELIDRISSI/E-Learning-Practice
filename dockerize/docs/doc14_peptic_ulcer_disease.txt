Peptic ulcer disease refers to sores in the lining of the stomach or duodenum caused by acid injury and impaired mucosal protection. The two major causes are Helicobacter pylori infection and nonsteroidal anti inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen or naproxen. Symptoms may include burning upper abdominal pain, nausea, bloating, early fullness, or pain related to meals, although some ulcers are silent. Complications can include bleeding, perforation, gastric outlet obstruction, and anemia. Warning signs include black stools, vomiting blood, fainting, severe sudden abdominal pain, and unexplained weight loss. Diagnosis may involve testing for H. pylori, endoscopy, blood counts, and stool testing for bleeding. Treatment includes stopping harmful NSAIDs when possible, acid suppression with proton pump inhibitors, and antibiotic combinations for H. pylori. Completing the full antibiotic course is important because incomplete treatment can lead to persistent infection. Some patients need confirmation that H. pylori has cleared. Alcohol and smoking can worsen healing. This document is educational only.
