Vaccination trains the immune system to recognize a pathogen or toxin without requiring natural infection. Vaccines may contain inactivated organisms, weakened organisms, protein fragments, polysaccharides, toxoids, viral vectors, or genetic instructions such as mRNA. After vaccination, immune cells produce antibodies and memory responses that can prevent disease or reduce severity. Community protection improves when enough people are immune, which lowers spread and protects those who cannot be vaccinated. Common vaccine side effects include soreness, mild fever, fatigue, or headache, usually resolving quickly. Serious reactions are rare but monitored through safety systems. Vaccine schedules are designed around age, exposure risk, pregnancy, immune status, travel, and occupational needs. Delaying vaccines can leave people vulnerable during high risk periods. Contraindications and precautions vary by vaccine, so clinicians review allergy history and immune conditions. Vaccination has reduced illnesses such as measles, polio, tetanus, diphtheria, influenza complications, and pneumococcal disease. This document supports education and does not replace medical advice.
