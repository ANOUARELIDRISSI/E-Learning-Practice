Gastroesophageal reflux disease, or GERD, occurs when stomach contents flow back into the esophagus and cause troublesome symptoms or complications. The lower esophageal sphincter normally helps prevent reflux, but it can relax inappropriately or become weakened. Common symptoms include heartburn, sour regurgitation, chest burning, chronic cough, hoarseness, throat clearing, and symptoms worse after meals or when lying down. Alarm features such as trouble swallowing, vomiting blood, black stools, unintentional weight loss, anemia, or persistent vomiting require medical evaluation. Lifestyle steps may include avoiding late meals, elevating the head of the bed, weight management, stopping tobacco, limiting trigger foods, reducing alcohol, and eating smaller meals. Medicines include antacids, H2 blockers, and proton pump inhibitors. Endoscopy may be used when symptoms are persistent, complicated, or associated with alarm signs. Untreated severe reflux can lead to esophagitis, strictures, Barrett esophagus, or dental irritation. This document is educational and not personal medical advice.
