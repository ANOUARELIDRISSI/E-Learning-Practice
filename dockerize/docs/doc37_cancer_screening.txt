Cancer screening looks for cancer or precancerous changes before symptoms appear. The goal is to find disease early enough to reduce illness or death, but screening also has limits such as false positives, false negatives, overdiagnosis, anxiety, and procedure risks. Screening recommendations depend on age, sex, anatomy, family history, genetics, personal medical history, and risk factors. Common examples include cervical cancer screening with HPV testing or Pap testing, breast cancer screening with mammography, colorectal cancer screening with stool tests or colonoscopy, lung cancer screening with low dose CT for selected smoking histories, and skin checks for high risk patients. Shared decision making helps match screening to a person's values and risk. A positive screening test does not always mean cancer; it usually means more evaluation is needed. Symptoms such as unexplained weight loss, persistent bleeding, new lumps, or changing moles should be evaluated even outside screening schedules. This document is educational and not individualized screening advice.

For RAG practice, useful retrieval clues include condition name, symptoms, risk factors, complications, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment.
